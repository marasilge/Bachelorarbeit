    In this work, if not specifized in any other way, every space has a topology and every map is a continuous map.  

\subsection{Motivation}
    The goal of this thesis is to formalize the \emph{homotopy extension property (HEP)} in Lean. I started with the proof of the \emph{retraction criterion} and worked towards the result that every relative CW complex satisfies the HEP. Our motivation for studying this property is that it is needed for two major theorems in algebraic topology. The first is the \emph{cellular approximation theorem}.

\begin{theorem}[Cellular Approximation Theorem, {\cite[Theorem 4.8]{MR1867354}}] 
    Every map $f \colon X \to Y $ of CW complexes is homotopic to a cellular map. If $f$ is already cellular on a subcomplex $A \subset X$, the homotopy may be taken to be stationary on A.
\end{theorem}
    
    Here a map is called \emph{cellular} if it sends the $n$-skeleton of $X$ to the $n$-skeleton of $Y$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$. \\
    Equipping $S^k$ with the CW structure consisting of one $0$-cell and one $k$-cell, it has no cells in dimensions $0 < n < k$. Hence any map $S^n \to S^k$ with $n < k$ is homotopic to one landing in the $n$-skeleton, which is a single point, and is therefore nullhomotopic. This yields the corollary $\pi_n(S^k) = 0$ for all $n < k$.

    Secondly, the result that relative CW complexes satisfy the HEP is one of the main steps towards the proof of \emph{Whitehead's theorem}.

{\emergencystretch=2em
\begin{theorem}[Whitehead's Theorem, {\cite[Theorem 4.5]{MR1867354}}] 
    If a map $f \colon X \to Y$ between connected CW complexes induces isomorphisms $f_* \colon \pi_n (X) \to  \pi _n (Y) $ for all $n$, then $f$ is a homotopy equivalence. In case $f$ is the inclusion of a subcomplex $X \hookrightarrow Y$, the conclusion is stronger: $X$ is a deformation retract of $Y$.
\end{theorem}
\par}
    
   Whitehead's theorem states that, in order to prove that two connected CW complexes are homotopy equivalent, it suffices to exhibit a map between them that induces isomorphisms on all homotopy groups, i.e.\ a weak homotopy equivalence. Showing that two spaces are homotopy equivalent is in general a difficult task, and Whitehead's theorem provides a powerful tool for such problems.

% https://www.math.uni-bielefeld.de/~fw/at.pdf
\begin{definition} \label{def:HEPDef}
A pair of topological spaces $(X,A)$ with $A \subseteq X$ has the
\emph{homotopy extension property (HEP)} if for every topological space $Y$, every
map $f \colon X \to Y$, and every homotopy $H \colon A \times [0,1] \to Y$ satisfying
$H(a,0) = f(a)$ for all $a \in A$, there exists a homotopy
$H' \colon X \times [0,1] \to Y$ such that
\begin{align*}
    H'(x,0) &= f(x)    && \text{for all } x \in X, \\
    H'(a,t) &= H(a,t)  && \text{for all } (a,t) \in A \times [0,1].
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    Note, that for any space $X$, the pair $(X,X)$ and the pair $(X ,\emptyset)$ have the \emph{HEP}.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
    A pair of two maps $(f,H)$, which agree on the space $A$ respectively $A\times \{ 0\} $, same as in the previous definition, is an \emph{extension problem}. 
\end{definition}

\begin{remark} \label{rm:HepDef}
    Another equivalent way of phrasing this definition is to say that $(X,A)$ has the \emph{HEP} if and only if every map defined on $X \cup_A (A \times [0,1])$ extends to a map on $X \times [0,1]$. Note that this is, in general, not the same as requiring that every map defined on $X \times \{0\} \cup (A \times [0,1])$ extends to a map on $X \times [0,1]$. This is because the natural map
    \[
    X \cup_A (A \times [0,1]) \to X \times \{0\} \cup (A \times [0,1])
    \]
    is not necessarily a homeomorphism. In general, it is only a continuous bijection. However, if $A$ is closed, then this map is a homeomorphism and therefore yields another equivalent definition. 
\end{remark}

The equivalece from \cref{rm:HepDef} is proofen in the following lemma.

\begin{lemma} \label{lem:lemma1}
    Let $A$ be a subset of a topological space $X$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
        \item $(X,A)$ has the \emph{HEP}
        \item for every topological space $Y$ and every continuous map $g\colon X \times \{ 0\} \cup A \times [0,1] \rightarrow Y$ there exists an extension of $g$ to a map $G : X \times [0,1] \rightarrow Y$
    \end{enumerate}
    Then $(i) \implies (ii)$ and under the additional condition, that $A$ is closed, also the inverse direction $(ii) \implies (i)$ holds.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof} \textbf{(i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii):}
    Let $g: X \times \{ 0\} \cup A \times [0,1] \rightarrow Y$. We define maps $f \colon X \rightarrow Y, x \mapsto g(x,0)$ and $ H \colon A \times [0,1] \rightarrow Y, (x,t) \mapsto g(x,t) $. It is easy to check, that $f$ and $H$ agree on $A \times [0,1]$, so the \emph{HEP} for the pair $(X,A)$ yields an extension of those two maps. We can chose $G$ to be exactly this extended homotopy.\\
    \textbf{($A$ closed $\land$ (ii)  ) $\Rightarrow$ (i):}
    Let $f \colon X \rightarrow Y$ and $ H \colon A \times [0,1] \rightarrow Y$. We define the map 
    \[
    g \colon X \times \{ 0\} \cup A \times [0,1] \rightarrow Y, \\
    (x,t) \mapsto \begin{cases}
        f(x), & \text{if } t = 0, \\
        H(x,t), & \text{else}
        \end{cases}
    \]
    Clearly $g$ is well-defined, since $f$ and $H$ agree on $A$. To proof continuity of $g$, we need that $A \subseteq X$ is closed: \\
    $A \subseteq X \text{ closed} \implies A \times [0,1] \subset X \times [0,1] \text{ closed} $. Furthermore, $X \times \{ 0\} \subseteq X \times [0,1]$ is closed. So $g$ is continuous, because it is continuous on two closed subsets and therefor also on their union. \\
    By assumption there exists an extension $G : X \times [0,1]$ of $g$, which we can choose to be the extended homotopy for the definition of the \emph{HEP}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma} \label{lem:lemma2}
    Let $A$ be a subset of a topological space $X$. Then the following are equivalent:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
        \item for every $g \colon A \rightarrow Y$ there exists an extension $G \colon X \rightarrow Y$ 
        \item $A$ is a retract of $X$, i.e. $\exists r : X \rightarrow A$, such that $r$ is the identity on $A \subset X$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    \textbf{(i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii):}
    We look at the special case of $(i)$, when $Y = A$ and $g = id_A$. Then the extension $G$ is the desiered retraction. \\
    \textbf{(ii) $\Rightarrow$ (i):} Let $g \colon A \rightarrow Y$. Then we define $G \colon X \rightarrow Y $ to be the map $g$ precomposed with a retraction $r \colon X \rightarrow A$. Since $r$ is the identity on $A$ this indeed is an extension of $g$. 
\end{proof}

To proof, that a space pair actually satisfies the \emph{HEP} it is a convenient way to use the \emph{retraction criterion}. This follows from the previous two lemma. Often, the retraction criterion is only phrased for closed subsets $A \subset X$, but I assumed the clossness condition on $A$, as in \cref{lem:lemma1}, only for the implication, where it is needed.

\begin{definition}\label{retraction}
    Let $X$ be a topological space and $A \subset X$ a subset. A retraction is a continuous map $r : X \to A$, such that precomposition with the inclusion $ i : A \hookrightarrow X $ yields the identity on $A$: $r \circ i : A \to A = id_A $. 
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Retraction Criterion] \label{cor:RetractionCriterion}
    Let $X$ be a topological space and $A \subseteq X$ a subspace. Consider the following two statements:
    \begin{enumerate}
    \renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
        \item the pair $(X,A)$ has the \emph{HEP}
        \item the subspace $X \times \{ 0\} \cup A \times [0,1] \subseteq X \times [0,1] $ is a retract of $X \times [0,1]$
    \end{enumerate}
    Then $(1) \implies (2)$. If, in addition, $A$ is closed in $X$, the converse $(2) \implies (1)$ holds as well.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    \textbf{(i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii):} If $(X,A)$ has the \emph{HEP}, then by "(i) $\implies$ (ii)" from \cref{lem:lemma1} we get: for every map $g \colon X \times \{ 0\} \cup A \times [0,1] \rightarrow Y$ there exists an extension of $g$ to a map on $ X \times [0,1] $. Now the statement follows directly from \cref{lem:lemma1}. \\
    \textbf{($A$ closed $\land$ (ii)) $\Rightarrow$ (i):} For this direction we first use that $X \cup A \times [0,1] \subseteq X \times [0,1] $ is closed and apply the backward direction of \cref{lem:lemma2}. This yields the same intermediate step as in the previous prart of this proof / the forward direction. With the same closedness as before, we apply the backward direction of \cref{lem:lemma1} and conclude the proof. 
\end{proof}

It follows from the retraction criterion, that the \emph{HEP} is preserved under taking the product with a space.

\begin{corollary} 
    If $(X,A)$ has the \emph{HEP}, so does $(W \times X, W \times A)$ for any space $W$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    If $r \colon X \times [0,1] \rightarrow X \times \{ 0\} \cup A \times [0,1] \subseteq X \times [0,1] $ is a retract, so is 
    \[
    id_W \times r \colon W \times X \times [0,1] \rightarrow W \times X \times \{ 0\} \cup W \times A \times [0,1] \subseteq W \times X \times [0,1] \text{.}
    \] 
    Continuity follows, because $id_W$ is the product of two continuous maps. Furthermore $id_W$ is fixed, where required, since $id_W$ is fixed on all of $W$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
    The \emph{HEP} is preserved under homeomorphisms, i.e. given two space pairs $(X,A) \text{and} (Y, B)$. If there exists a homeomorphism from $X$ to $Y$, that maps  $A$ to $B$, and $(X,A)$ has the \emph{HEP}, then so does $(Y,B)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\phi \colon X \to Y$, be a homeomorphism, that maps  $A$ to $B$ and let $r_X \colon X \times [0,1] \to X \times \{ 0\} \cup A \times [0,1]$ be a retraction, which we obtain from the Retraction Criterion for the pair $(X,A)$. $pr_X \colon X \times [0,1] \to X$ and $pr_I \colon X \times [0,1] \to X$ are the projections onto the first respectively second component. We use those maps, to define $r_Y$. 
\begin{align*}
    r_Y \colon Y \times [0,1] &\rightarrow Y \times \{ 0\} \cup B \times [0,1] \\
    (y,t) & \mapsto (\phi \circ pr_X \circ r_X (\phi^{-1} (y), t), pr_I \circ r_X (\phi^{-1} (y), t))
\end{align*}
    It is left to check, that $r_Y$ is a retraction, which yields the \emph{HEP} for the space pair $(Y,B)$. The map $r_Y$ ... \\
\begin{itemize}
    \item is continuous: the two component functions are compositions of continuous maps.
    \item is fixed on $ Y \times \{ 0\} \cup B \times [0,1] $: Let $(y,t)$ be a point in this subspace of $Y \times [0,1]$. Then, $(\phi^{-1} (y), t)$ is in $ X \times \{ 0\} \cup A \times [0,1] $, because $ \phi^{-1} $ maps $Y$ to $X$ and $B$ to $A$. So $(\phi^{-1} (y), t)$ lies in exactly the space, which is fixed under $r_X$. After applying $\phi$ onto the first component, we observe, that $r_Y (y,t) = (y,t)$.
    \item has image in $Y \times \{ 0\} \cup B \times [0,1]$: Let $(y,t)$ be a point in $Y \times [0,1]$. $r_X (\phi^{-1} (y), t)$ is in the image of $r_X $, which is $X \times \{ 0\} \cup A \times [0,1]$. It follows from the definition of $r_Y$, that either the second component of $r_Y (y,t)$ is zero, or the first one lies in B.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}


\subsection{HEP for relative CW-complexes}

To proof the \emph{HEP} for relative CW-complexes, we will generalize the space pair in three steps and proof the \emph{HEP} for each of these pairs. The general strategy is as follows. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Any disc relative its boundary has the \emph{HEP}. 
    \item The pair of two consecutive skeletons has the \emph{HEP}, i.e  $(X_{n+1},X_n)$ has the \emph{HEP} for all $n$.
    \item Any relative CW-comples has the \emph{HEP}.
\end{enumerate}

Before we start proofing the \emph{HEP} for any space pair, I will give some definitinons, introduce notation for CW-complexes and proof some basic properties for them, which are not yet in mathlib. \\
I copied the following definition of CW-complexes from the bachelor thesis of Hannah Scholz \cite{SCH01}. Her formalisation of CW complexes, which can be found as the "classical CW-complex" in mathlib, is explained in this thesis. She chose the historical definition of CW-complexes, presented by Whithead in \cite{WH}. 

\begin{definition}\label{defi:CWComplex2}
    Let $X$ be a Hausdorff space.
    A \emph{CW-complex} on $X$ consists of a family of indexing sets $(I_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ and a family of maps $(Q_i^n\colon D_i^n\rightarrow X)_{n \ge 0, i \in I_n}$ s.t.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\restrict{Q_i^n}{\interior{D_i^n}}\colon \interior{D_i^n} \rightarrow Q_i^n(\interior{D_i^n})$ is a homeomorphism. We call $\openCell{n}{i} \coloneq Q_i^n(\interior{D_i^n})$ an \emph{(open) $n$-cell} (or a cell of dimension $n$)
        and $\closedCell{n}{i} \coloneq Q_i^n(D_i^n)$ a \emph{closed $n$-cell}.
        \item For all $n, m \in \mathbb{N}$, $i \in I_n$ and $j \in I_m$ where $(n, i) \ne (m, j)$ the cells $\openCell{n}{i}$ and $\openCell{m}{j}$ are disjoint.
        \item For each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $i \in I_n$, $Q_i^n(\boundary D_i^n)$ is contained in the union of a finite number of closed cells of dimension less than $n$.
        \item $A \subseteq X$ is closed iff $Q_i^n(D_i^n) \cap A$ is closed for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $i \in I_n$.
        \item $\bigcup_{n \ge 0}\bigcup_{i \in I_n} Q_i^n(D_i^n) = X$.
    \end{enumerate}
    We call $Q_i^n$ a \emph{characteristic map} and $\cellFrontier{n}{i} \coloneq Q_i^n(\boundary D_i^n)$ the \emph{frontier of the $n$-cell} for any $i$ and $n$.
    Additionally we define $X_n \coloneq \bigcup_{m < n + 1} \bigcup_{i \in I_m} \closedCell{m}{i}$ and call it the \emph{$n$-skeleton} of $X$ for $-1 \le n \le \infty$
\end{definition}

\subsection{Junk Values} \label{junk values}
If you write a division by zero in a formalized theorem, Lean returns the result~0.
\begin{lstlisting}
example : 1 / 0 = 0 := by norm_num
\end{lstlisting}
This makes division in Lean always well-defined, even though certain inputs have no genuine mathematical meaning. As explained in \cite{Meiburg}, the use of so-called \emph{junk values} is common practice. The same convention applies, for instance, to the inversion of square matrices, which is always defined and returns the zero matrix whenever the matrix is singular, or to the derivative of a non-differentiable function, which is defined to be zero. Many further examples of this kind exist.
At first glance this may seem concerning, since such values have no mathematical justification and could appear to enable incorrect proofs. Indeed, one can state and prove propositions that, in classical mathematics, would look false or ill-defined. This issue is resolved by adding suitable hypotheses such as \lean{n $\neq$ 0} or \lean{Differentiable k f} to any statement about the object in question. They ensure we only use the result of the theorem in apllicable situations, such as, if our number is nonzero or our function is differentiable. Consequently, although the convention may seem alarming initially, relying on junk values is standard practice in Lean formalizations, precisely because it simplifies both definitions and statements. Returning to the first example, it is what allows division to be expressed as a total map $\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Q}$.

\subsection{Retraction Criterion}
Before elucidating the retraction criterion its formalisation, we look at the definition of a retraction. 
The classical way to define a retraction $r$ from $B$ to $A$ is, as in \cref{retraction}, via precomposition with the inclusion $A \hookrightarrow B$. As already in the formalisation of the definition \emph{HEP}, we again want to avoid working with subtypes. So insetad of defining a map from $B$ to $A$, for the formalisation, the retraction map is defined from an ambient type $X$ to itself. The structure \lean{RetractionOn} bundles all properties, which make this map a retraction. 
%  is the convenient approach, I chose a different phrasing for the formalization. Instead of working directly with the classical definition, I introduced a structure that bundles a map with two sets and three defining properties. Concretely, I defined the following structure:

\begin{lstlisting}
structure RetractionOn.{u_1} {X : Type u_1} [TopologicalSpace X] (r : X → X) (B A : Set X) : Prop where
  subset : A ⊆ B
  continuousOn : ContinuousOn r B
  mapsTo : B.MapsTo r A
  fixesOn : ∀ a ∈ A, r a = a
\end{lstlisting}

As mentioned before, that the map \lean{r} is a map from a Type $X$ into itself and it is not a retraction on all of its domain. As the name \lean{RetractionOn} implies, it just "retracts" a subset \lean{B} to a subset \lean{A} of the \lean{Type X}. The first property \lean{subset} states, that \lean{A} is a subset of \lean{B}. Note, that leaving out this property in our structure would define a more general class of selfmaps, which does not necessarily sattisfy $r(B) = A$ anymore. The property \lean{continuousOn} ensures, it is a continuous Map on the relevant subset $B$. The codomain of the retraction is supposed to be $A$, so the property \lean{mapsTo} claims, that \lean{r} maps all elements of \lean{B} to an element in \lean{A}. Furthermore the classical definition states, that precomposition with the inclusion $A \hookrightarrow B$ should give the identity $id_A$. In our case there is not really an inclusion map, since both - image and preimage of \lean{r} - are the same \lean{Type X}. So in our setting this condition translates to, that each element in A needs to be fixed by applying $r$. This property is called \lean{fixedOn}. \\

In the lean file you can again find the forward implication without closedness assumption.

\begin{lstlisting}
lemma retraction_criterion_closed'.{u} {Y : Type u} [TopologicalSpace Y] (A X : Set Y) (hAX : A ⊆ X) (hA1 : IsClosed A) :
  HEP' X A ↔ ∃ s : Y × ℝ → Y × ℝ, RetractionOn s {p | p.1 ∈ X ∧ p.2 ∈ unitInterval} {p | p.1 ∈ X ∧ p.2 = 0 ∨ p.1 ∈ A ∧ p.2 ∈ unitInterval}
\end{lstlisting}

I proved the \lean{retraction_criterion_closed'} using the \\ \lean{retraction_criterion_closed}. Explicitly, for the forward direction, I obtained a retraction \lean{r} as in ToDo. In order to extend this map \lean{r} to a map \lean{s : Y × ℝ → Y × ℝ}, I precomposed in the first component with a map called \lean{mapYX : Y → X }, which is the identity on the set \lean{X} and maps all other points of Y to an arbitrary junk value in the unitInterval. \lean{Subtype.val} sends \lean{(r x).1 ∈ X }, to the corresponding point in Y. Afterwards, I had to confirm this new defined map \lean{r} indeed is a retraction, i.e. it is continuous on the relevant set, maps to the required one and the necessary values remain unchanged under applying \lean{r}. \\
For the backward direction I start with a retraction \lean{s : Y × ℝ → Y × ℝ} and want to construct \lean{r : $\uparrow$X × ℝ → $\uparrow$X × ℝ}, so applying \lean{retractioncriterionclosed} yields, the claim. One might expect this direction to be easier, since we dont have to construct any additional values, but just restrict \lean{s}, to the desired space. Sadly these dreams shatter at the point, where we want the image to lie in $X \times I \subset Y \times \mathbb{R}$. \lean{s} is a retraction on %\lean{{p | p.1 ∈ X ∧ p.2 ∈ unitInterval}}
, so the \lean{RetractionOn.mapsTo} property gives no information about, where in $Y \times \mathbb{R}$ we are, if the second coordinate comes from outside the unit interval. I solved this issue by procomposing the real coordinate with the map \lean{c : ℝ → ℝ := fun t ↦ (Set.projIcc (0 : ℝ) 1 zero_le_one t)}. \lean{Set.projIcc}, is a continuous map from mathlib, which retracts a linear ordered Type to a closed interval in it. This precomoposition makes, that for every point, which we apply to \lean{s}, the \lean{mapsTo} property of \lean{s} is applicable. This extra work, is a downside of working with types instead of subtypes. It would have been avoided, if I defined a homotopy only on \lean{Y × unitInterval}, instead of \lean{Y × ℝ}. \\
I want to point out, that even tough the two versions of the retraction criterion are phrased with two different versions of the definition, this was more a choise based to style, than on technical need. Also, I experienced both of these versions to be valuable. While it might be advisable to just choose the definition \lean{HEP'}, I see usage in both versions of the retraction criterion. 
The usage of \lean{retractioncriterionclosed} is underlined by the the proof, that the pair of two consequitive skeletons satisfies the \emph{HEP}. I constructed a retraction \lean{r: (skeletonLT X m) $\times \ \mathbb{R} \to $ (skeletonLT X m) $\times \ \mathbb{R}$}. The key theorem for the construction of this continuous map was the lemma \lean{Topology.IsQuotientMap.continuous_lift_prod_left}, which is a formalisation of Whitehead's Theorem about products of quotient maps with a locally compact factor. If I worked with ambient spaces, I would have lost the surjectivity of the quotient map and would not have been able to apply this therem, the way it can be found in mathlib.

\subsection{Preservation of the HEP}
Besides the retraction criterion I formalised two more theorems, which are usefull to prove, that a pair of topological spaces has the \emph{HEP}. One is, that the \emph{HEP} is preserved under homeomorphisms. The other is sort of a transitivity property, i.e. if $(A1,A2)$ and $(A2,A2)$ are pairs of topological spaces and both of them satisfy the \emph{HEP}, then also the pair $(A1,A3)$ has the \emph{HEP}. \\

\subsection{HEP for discs and cubes}

The goal of the file \lean{HEP_ball_cube.lean } is, to prove the homotopy extension property for balls and cubes relative to their boundary. We start with the balls: As in the prove of \cref{thm:HepDisc}, I first constructed the auxilliar functionion \lean{auxfun} combining the information of the given maps \lean{f} and \lean{H}. Then this function is used to explicitly define a desired homotopy \lean{H'}, which we need to check the definition fo \lean{HEP}

The continuity of \lean{H'} follows from composing two \lean{ContinuousOn} functions. The proofs for all desired properties of \lean{H'} are not hard.
